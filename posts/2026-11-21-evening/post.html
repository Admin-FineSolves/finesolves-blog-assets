How big is warehousing demand in India in 2026?

Warehousing demand in India is at a record: 36.2 million sq ft of logistics and industrial leasing in H1 2026, according to Cushman & Wakefield. That was 18% higher than H1 2025, and warehouses made up 24.3 million sq ft, or 67% of total absorption.

Third-party logistics (3PL) firms led, with 12.2 million sq ft and a 34% share, after 64% year-on-year growth. Engineering and manufacturing occupiers took 10.2 million sq ft, or 28%. JLL's Q1 2026 data points the same way: leasing rose 13% to 18.3 million sq ft, and warehousing was 72% of absorption.

Vestian's Q3 data shows the run continuing. Absorption reached 10.58 million sq ft in July–September 2026, up 15% year on year, taking the nine-month total to 32.6 million sq ft. Rents across the seven cities it tracks ranged from ₹20 to ₹29 per sq ft per month, and the sector drew $332 million of institutional investment in the quarter, 17% of all real estate investment.

How much space is being built, and how good is it?

India's eight largest markets hold 514 million sq ft of industrial and warehousing stock, and JLL expects 850 million sq ft by 2030. Grade A space is 293 million sq ft, or 57% of the total, JLL reports.

JLL expects stock to grow at an 11.4% CAGR to get there, after 73.7 million sq ft of gross absorption in 2025. Grade A buildings matter for automation: flat floors, higher clear heights and better power make racking, conveyors and mobile robots practical. In an older, low-ceiling shed, the first automation step is almost always software, not hardware.

Why is warehouse automation in India rising now?

Three pressures are converging: rising labour costs, tougher service levels from quick commerce and 3PL contracts, and buildings finally fit for equipment. The data on each is clear.

On labour, Zebra Technologies' Warehousing Vision Study cites a 7% compound annual rise in warehouse labour costs through the decade. On service, 51% of operators globally, and 45% in APAC including India, say maintaining fill rates is getting harder. CBRE expects India's quick-commerce gross order value to reach ₹2 trillion by 2028, driving dense micro-fulfilment centres that hold roughly 2,000–4,000 SKUs and cannot afford picking errors.

Investment intent is high. Zebra found 64% of operators globally and 63% in APAC plan to raise spending on warehouse modernisation over five years.

What are supply chain leaders actually spending on?

They are funding AI and robotics together, with more than half planning seven-figure budgets. The MHI and Deloitte 2026 Annual Industry Report found 52% plan to spend over $1 million on supply chain innovation, and 17% over $10 million.

The same survey shows why. 71% of respondents say AI is disrupting supply chains, and 24% expect that disruption to be transformational. 48% rate AI's impact as significant or greater, and robotics and automation ranks second, with 39% rating it significant or greater. 56% expect to raise innovation spending overall.

Gartner's longer view is more aggressive. It predicts that by 2030, 50% of new warehouses in developed markets will be designed as robot-centric facilities where humans are optional. It is also sceptical of the flashiest options: Gartner expects fewer than 20 companies to have humanoid robots live in production for supply chain and manufacturing through 2028. Task-specific robots are where the money is going.

Does warehouse automation pay back?

It can, quickly, when it targets a measurable loss such as inventory errors, but the best-known numbers come from vendor-commissioned models. Read them as an upper bound and rebuild them with your own costs.

A Forrester Total Economic Impact study, commissioned by robotics vendor Dexory, modelled a composite global 3PL running autonomous inventory-scanning robots across three warehouses. It found a 219% three-year ROI and payback in under six months. Benefits totalled $4.3 million against $1.35 million in risk-adjusted costs, for a net present value of $2.96 million. The largest benefit lines were lower stockholding costs, fewer lost pallets and automated cycle counting.

The lesson for Indian operators is where the value sits: in data accuracy, not in replacing pickers. At ₹20 to ₹29 per sq ft per month, a pallet position lost to a wrong location or phantom stock is real rent paid for nothing.

Where do Indian warehouses sit on the automation curve?

Most are early. An Alvarez & Marsal report placed most Indian warehouses at Levels 0 to 2 on a five-level scale, from fully manual to end-to-end automation.

That report, from 2023, expected over 80% of Indian warehouses to have some level of automation by 2030. In practice, "some level" usually starts with barcode scanning and a warehouse management system, then adds conveyors, sortation or autonomous mobile robots in the highest-volume zones. The jump from Level 1 to Level 2 is mostly software and process; the jump to Level 3 and above is capital equipment.

How to plan a warehouse automation programme: a 6-step playbook

  1. Baseline the loss. Measure picks per hour, order accuracy, inventory accuracy, dock-to-stock time and cost per order for four weeks. Without a baseline, no ROI claim will survive the CFO.
  2. Fix the data layer first. Put every location, SKU and movement into a warehouse management system with barcode or RFID capture. Robots running on bad master data move errors faster.
  3. Integrate before you automate. Connect the WMS to ERP, transport and marketplace order feeds through APIs, so inventory is one number everywhere.
  4. Add AI where decisions repeat. Slotting, wave planning, labour forecasting and replenishment triggers are software problems that pay back without new hardware.
  5. Pilot warehouse robotics in one zone. Pick the highest-volume or highest-error zone, set a 90-day target against the baseline, and keep a manual fallback.
  6. Scale on evidence. Expand only the pilots that beat the baseline, and renegotiate 3PL or customer SLAs around the new accuracy you can prove.

What this means for Indian businesses

For Indian manufacturers, distributors and 3PLs, warehouse automation in 2026 is less about robots and more about trustworthy stock data. The real decisions are which loss to attack first, and whether your systems can tell you the truth about stock. With leasing at a record and quick commerce pushing service levels, the operators that win the next contract will be the ones who can prove accuracy with data.

Frequently asked questions

What is warehouse automation?

Warehouse automation is the use of software and equipment to run warehouse tasks with less manual effort. It ranges from a warehouse management system with barcode scanning, through conveyors and sortation, to autonomous mobile robots and fully robot-centric sites. Most Indian warehouses sit at the early, largely manual end of that scale, according to an Alvarez & Marsal assessment.

How big is the warehouse automation market in India?

CBRE's India Logistics Market Outlook 2026 projects the Indian warehouse automation market to reach $1.42 billion by 2030. Demand is driven by record leasing of Grade A space, quick-commerce growth and rising labour costs. Treat any single market-size figure as a directional estimate rather than a precise forecast.

What is the ROI of warehouse automation?

It depends on the loss you target. A Forrester study commissioned by Dexory modelled a 219% three-year ROI and payback under six months for inventory-scanning robots at a composite 3PL. That is a vendor-commissioned model, so rebuild the business case with your own baseline of errors, labour and rent before committing capital.

Should a mid-sized Indian warehouse start with robots or software?

Start with software. A warehouse management system, barcode capture and clean integration with ERP and order channels fix most accuracy problems at a fraction of the cost of hardware. Once inventory data is reliable and you have a measured baseline, pilot robotics in the single zone where volume or error rates are highest.

Which Indian cities lead warehousing demand in 2026?

Delhi NCR led H1 2026 with 8.7 million sq ft of logistics and industrial leasing, according to Cushman & Wakefield, followed by Chennai at 6.1 million and Pune at 5.9 million sq ft. In Q3 2026, Vestian data showed Chennai on top, so city rankings shift quarter to quarter.
