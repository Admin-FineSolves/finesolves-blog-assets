Technical debt cost is the extra money and time a business pays because its software was built with shortcuts, outdated parts or tangled integrations. Protiviti's global survey of technology executives found organisations spend an average of 30% of their IT budgets servicing technical debt, and McKinsey's CIO survey puts the debt itself at 20% to 40% of the value of the entire technology estate.

This matters more in 2026 than it did five years ago because AI projects sit on top of old systems. An IDC study for MongoDB found 46% of Indian organisations say their current architecture makes it impossible to build new applications without extensive modernisation. Every rupee spent working around that architecture is a rupee not spent on the AI, analytics or customer features the board keeps asking about. This post pulls the hard benchmarks together so you can size your own debt.

What is technical debt cost, in plain numbers?

Technical debt cost is the ongoing "interest" you pay on past software shortcuts: slower changes, more outages, more manual workarounds and higher run costs. The principal is the work needed to fix the code itself.

McKinsey surveyed 50 CIOs at large financial-services and technology companies and found they estimate tech debt at 20% to 40% of the value of their whole technology estate, before depreciation. The same survey found 10% to 20% of the technology budget meant for new products gets diverted to resolving tech debt instead. In McKinsey's 2023 follow-up, 30% of CIOs said more than 20% of their new-product budget is diverted this way.

At national scale the numbers get large. Accenture, citing a 2022 CISQ report, says tech debt costs the US $2.41 trillion a year and would require $1.52 trillion to fix. India has no equivalent official estimate, which is itself a warning sign: most Indian firms have never measured theirs.

How much of the IT budget goes to technical debt?

Roughly a third: Protiviti's survey of more than 1,000 technology leaders found organisations spend an average of 30% of their IT budgets on technical debt, plus 20% of their resources on managing it.

The spread by sector and country is wide. Protiviti reports that transportation and logistics firms spend 39% of their IT budget servicing technical debt, and UK organisations dedicate 38%. Compare that with Accenture's finding that leading companies deliberately target about 15% of the IT budget for tech debt remediation, from its digital core survey of 1,500 companies. The gap between 30% spent reactively and 15% spent on purpose is the real benchmark: the leaders pay less because they pay on a schedule.

Why does technical debt hit Indian firms harder in the AI era?

Because AI needs clean data and flexible systems, and most Indian firms run neither: IDC found 46% of Indian organisations say their architecture blocks new applications without extensive modernisation.

The same IDC study, commissioned by MongoDB and published in April 2026, found support for new AI initiatives was the top driver for modernising databases and applications in India, cited by 50% of firms. It also found almost all organisations (98%) have experienced a failed modernisation initiative, with siloed and poor-quality data as the main obstacle. IDC predicts that organisations that ignore technical debt will face 50% higher failure rates for their AI initiatives by 2027.

Accenture's research points the same way: 41% of executives named AI as the single highest contributor to new tech debt, level with enterprise applications. AI is both the reason to pay down debt and a fresh source of it.

The IDC study lists the top software development challenges for Indian teams: embedding security without slowing delivery (35%), data management and poor-quality data (34%), and gaps between software teams and the business (28%). Among Indian "leaders", 57% run multiple programmes to keep chipping away at legacy constraints, and IDC reports those leaders earn more than 3x the share of digital revenue: 73% against 20% for mainstream peers.

Does paying down technical debt actually pay back?

Yes, on the available evidence: IBM's Institute for Business Value found that accounting for technical debt can boost AI returns by 29%, and Accenture links low debt to faster revenue growth.

Accenture reports that companies with lower-than-average tech debt expect 5.3% revenue growth over 2024 to 2026, against 4.4% for peers. McKinsey found firms in the bottom 20th percentile on tech debt severity are 40% more likely to have incomplete or cancelled IT modernisations than those in the top 20th percentile. One cloud provider's CIO told McKinsey that engineer time lost to the tech debt "tax" fell from 75% to 25% after a structured clean-up. McKinsey also estimates 71% of the impact of a business transformation depends on technology, so debt that slows technology slows the whole programme.

How do you measure technical debt cost in your own systems?

Measure three things within a month: IT spend on maintenance and fixes, engineer time on unplanned work, and the run cost of your worst ten systems.

McKinsey's work suggests the debt is concentrated: typically 10 to 15 assets drive the majority of an enterprise's tech debt, and debt levels vary two to three times between applications. That means you do not need a full code audit to begin. Protiviti found only 6% of technology organisations have a process to track and report technical debt, so even a rough register puts you ahead of most peers.

How much should you budget for tech debt remediation?

A planned 15% of the IT budget is the benchmark leading companies use, according to Accenture, but McKinsey warns a flat carve-out alone is not enough without targeting.

McKinsey argues that simply ring-fencing 15% to 20% of IT budget is insufficient if the money is spread thinly; it should go to the few assets that cause most of the drag. Its example of a large financial services group shows the upside of focus: during a core system upgrade the group eliminated 94% of its customisations and saved double-digit millions of dollars. Start-ups in McKinsey's research dedicate two sprint cycles every quarter to clean-up, which is a useful rhythm for mid-sized Indian tech teams too.

A 6-step playbook to cut technical debt cost

  1. Build a debt register. List your top 15 systems with run cost, incident count and change lead time. Most of the debt will sit in a handful.
  2. Measure the interest. Track the share of engineer hours on unplanned fixes for one quarter. If it looks anything like the 75% McKinsey's cloud provider started at, you have your business case.
  3. Ring-fence a fixed share. Move towards the 15% of IT budget leaders allocate, and protect it from feature deadlines.
  4. Fix data before AI. IDC's 34% data-quality finding says clean pipelines come before any model work.
  5. Decide rebuild versus repair per system. Where debt exceeds half the asset's value, price a rebuild alongside the patch plan; our custom software teams scope both before recommending either.
  6. Stop adding new debt. Require architecture review for AI pilots, since 41% of executives see AI as the biggest new source of debt.

What this means for Indian businesses

For an Indian manufacturer, distributor or hospital chain, technical debt cost rarely shows up as a line item. It hides in an old ERP customisation nobody dares touch, in Excel sheets that bridge two systems, and in the three people who know how the billing module works. With 46% of Indian firms saying their architecture blocks new applications and 50% modernising mainly to support AI, the firms that measure and schedule their debt will ship AI faster than those that keep patching.

Frequently asked questions

What is technical debt in simple terms?

Technical debt is the future cost created when software is built quickly, cheaply or with outdated components. Like financial debt it has a principal, the work to fix the code, and interest, the extra time and money every later change costs. McKinsey's CIO survey estimates it at 20% to 40% of a company's technology estate value.

How much does technical debt cost a company?

Protiviti's survey of more than 1,000 technology leaders found companies spend an average of 30% of IT budgets servicing technical debt, rising to 39% in transportation and logistics. McKinsey adds that 10% to 20% of new-product technology budgets get diverted to fixing old problems instead of building new ones.

What percentage of IT budget should go to tech debt remediation?

Accenture's research on 1,500 companies found leading firms allocate about 15% of their IT budget to tech debt remediation on a planned basis. McKinsey cautions that a flat budget is not enough on its own: the money should target the small number of systems that cause most of the cost.

Does technical debt affect AI projects?

Yes. IDC predicts organisations that do not address technical debt will face 50% higher failure rates for AI initiatives by 2027, and IBM's Institute for Business Value found accounting for tech debt can lift AI returns by 29%. Poor data quality, cited by 34% of Indian teams, is the usual link between the two.

Should we rebuild or refactor a legacy system?

McKinsey suggests considering a greenfield rebuild when a unit's tech debt exceeds 50% of its technology asset value, and treating more than half of project budgets going to integrations and legacy fixes as a spiral warning. Below those levels, targeted refactoring of the worst components is usually cheaper and less risky.
