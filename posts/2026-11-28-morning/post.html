India's AI skills gap is not a shortage of course sign-ups. It is a gap in what workers learn alongside AI. Coursera's Global Skills Report 2026, released this week, ranks India 40th of 98 countries on a new index of AI and human skills, even though Indian AI micro-credential enrolments grew 181% against 122% globally.

This matters now because Indian firms are moving AI from pilots into daily operations, and the people running those systems need judgment as much as tool skills. The report lands as Bain & Company projects more AI job openings than India's talent pool can fill by 2027. Below: what the report found, where India leads and lags, how it fits with the EY and Bain numbers, and a practical plan for an ops or IT head closing the AI skills gap inside one company.

TL;DR

  • India ranks 40th of 98 countries and 12th in Asia Pacific on Coursera's new AI-human skills index, built from more than 300 million learners' data.
  • Indian AI micro-credential enrolments rose 181% year on year, ahead of 122% globally, while overall AI course enrolments grew 57% against 67% worldwide.
  • India trails on the newest skills: AI security courses grew 296% here versus 665% globally, and agentic workflows 238% versus 546%.
  • Human skills lag most: critical thinking enrolments grew 65% in India against 107% globally, and complex problem-solving 55% against 126%.
  • Bain & Company estimates India's AI job openings will pass 2.3 million by 2027, while the talent pool may reach just 1.2 million.

What did the Coursera Global Skills Report 2026 find about India?

It found that India learns AI at scale but builds the human skills that make AI useful more slowly, placing it 40th of 98 countries on the new index.

The Coursera Global Skills Report 2026 is the eighth edition of the series, according to CXOtoday. Business Standard reports that it combines learning data from more than 300 million Coursera and Udemy learners with Oxford Insights' Government AI Readiness Index 2025. India placed 40th globally and 12th in Asia Pacific on the report's new AI-human skills index, which measures whether a country builds AI skills and human skills together.

The headline volume figure is strong. Learners in India enrolled in GenAI content at 12 enrolments per minute in 2026, up from six per minute in 2025, the report shows. India leads on volume. It does not lead on balance.

Where does India lead, and where does it trail?

India leads on short, job-focused AI credentials and trails the world on the newest technical topics and on human skills such as critical thinking.

AI micro-credential enrolments in India grew 181%, well above the global 122%, according to Business Standard's reading of the report. That is the one category where India clearly outpaces the world. Overall AI course enrolments grew 57%, below the global rate of 67%, Inside AI reported from the same report.

The newer the topic, the wider the gap. AI security enrolments grew 296% in India against 665% globally, and agentic workflows grew 238% against 546%, Inside AI found. On generative AI the two are close, at 125% in India and 132% globally. For companies that are about to put AI agents into procurement, customer service or finance workflows, the slow uptake of security and agent design skills is the number to watch.

Why do human skills matter for the AI skills gap?

Because AI tools produce drafts, forecasts and recommendations, and someone still has to judge whether they are right before they reach a customer, a supplier or a balance sheet.

The report shows the sharpest gap here. Critical thinking enrolments in India grew 65% against 107% globally. Complex problem-solving grew 55% against 126%, and innovation 62% against 74%, according to Business Standard. In other words, Indian learners are adding tool skills faster than the judgment needed to use those tools well.

For an operations team, this shows up in familiar ways. A planner accepts a demand forecast without checking it against a known festival spike. A support agent pastes an AI answer that quotes the wrong return policy. A finance analyst trusts an AI-built reconciliation that silently dropped rows. None of these is a tool failure. Each is a review failure.

How big is India's AI talent shortage?

Bain & Company estimates AI job openings in India will pass 2.3 million by 2027, while the AI talent pool may grow to only 1.2 million.

That projection, reported by ThePrint in March 2025, implies demand running well ahead of supply. The same report says demand for AI-related skills has grown 21% a year since 2019, and pay for AI professionals has risen 11% a year. It also says 44% of executives report that a lack of in-house AI expertise is slowing them down.

Other estimates point the same way. The India Skills Report 2026 says India holds 16% of the global AI talent pool, expected to reach 1.25 million by 2027, according to Careers360. EY India estimates that GenAI could transform 38 million Indian jobs by 2030, a figure the Coursera report also cites. The supply is large in absolute terms. It is small next to the number of roles that will change.

Is the AI skills gap slowing AI adoption in Indian companies?

Yes, on the evidence available: talent is the most cited barrier, and most Indian enterprises have not yet moved GenAI into production.

EY's AIdea of India 2025 survey of more than 125 C-suite leaders found that only 3% of Indian enterprises had enough in-house talent and resources for GenAI, and 97% named lack of talent as a primary hurdle. Only 15% had GenAI in production, and 36% had not started experimenting. EY also estimated that 24% of tasks across industries could be fully automated and another 42% enhanced by AI.

Use is spreading regardless. The India Skills Report 2026 says more than 90% of Indian employees have started working with generative AI tools. Coursera's employer survey found nearly 60% of surveyed companies with active AI deployment and 88% of surveyed employees using AI at work, Inside AI reported. People are using AI before companies have trained them to check it. That is the gap in practice.

Should companies hire AI talent or upskill existing staff?

Most mid-market Indian firms should do both, but upskilling the people who already know the business usually closes the operational gap faster than hiring.

Employers already favour job-focused credentials. Coursera's employer survey found 95% of surveyed Indian employers value industry-partnered micro-credentials over institution-only ones, and 81% say candidates with micro-credentials move through hiring faster, according to Inside AI. Wider employability is also improving: the India Skills Report 2026 puts national employability at 56.35%, up from 54.81% a year earlier.

Hiring makes sense for a few specialist roles: someone who owns data pipelines, someone who owns model evaluation and security, and a product owner for each AI workflow. Everyone else who touches an AI output needs structured AI upskilling in how to review it. From the way we build AI systems for clients, the review step is where most of the value is kept or lost.

A 6-step plan to close the AI skills gap in your team

  1. Map AI touchpoints. List every workflow where an AI output reaches a customer, supplier, regulator or ledger. That list is your training scope.
  2. Name a reviewer per workflow. Each AI output gets a named human owner who signs off. Write down what "correct" looks like for that output.
  3. Pair tool training with judgment training. For every hour on prompting or a new AI feature, spend time on worked examples of AI errors from your own data.
  4. Add security and agent basics. Anyone configuring AI agents needs training in access control, prompt injection and audit logs. The report shows these are the skills India is slowest to build.
  5. Use short credentials with a test. Pick industry-partnered micro-credentials and add an internal practical test on your real workflows.
  6. Measure review quality, not course completions. Track error rates caught before release and time to correct. Completion certificates alone do not tell you whether the AI skills gap is closing.

What this means for Indian businesses

The Coursera Global Skills Report 2026 confirms two things operators already sense. Indian teams are keen to learn AI, and they are learning the tools faster than the judgment and security habits that make AI safe to run. With Bain projecting a gap between 2.3 million openings and a 1.2 million talent pool, few firms will hire their way out.

The practical response is to design AI systems around the people you have: clear review steps, narrow agent permissions, audit trails and training on your own failure cases. Government programmes help at the edges; the IndiaAI Mission's YUVA AI for ALL targets 10 million people, Inside AI reports. Inside a company, the work is specific to each workflow.
