Chariot-1 is also one of the first results from a ₹10,372 crore public programme, so it is a test of whether subsidised compute turns into models that operators can actually use. What is Chariot-1?

Chariot-1 is a suite of two foundation models, an LLM and a text-to-speech model, pre-trained from scratch in India by Chariot Artificial Intelligence Pvt. Ltd. with IndiaAI Mission support.

MeitY Secretary S. Krishnan launched the models at Bharat Mandapam in New Delhi, according to the Press Information Bureau release dated 26 November 2026. Chariot was among the first four teams selected under the IndiaAI Mission to build sovereign foundation models. The LLM is an 11-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model that activates 4 billion parameters per token, which keeps inference cost closer to a small model than a large one. It comes in three sizes: 10.7 billion, 1.7 billion and 745 million parameters.

The speech model generates English plus Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Gujarati, Punjabi and Odia. That list covers most of the languages an Indian customer-support or field-operations team actually meets.

How does the Chariot-1 tokeniser cut costs?

It packs Indian text into fewer tokens, so the same sentence costs less to process and leaves more room in the context window.

The tokeniser, called IndicShift, maps 1,012 characters across 12 Indian scripts onto a shared alphabet of 350 letters, the PIB release says. The result, as reported by the developer, is up to 2.5x fewer tokens on Indian text than OpenAI's GPT tokeniser. The company also reports a 5% to 8% improvement in model performance on Indian languages for the same compute.

Token count is the unit most LLM APIs bill on, and it also sets how much of a long document fits in one prompt. If a Hindi claims note or a Tamil delivery complaint takes far more tokens than its English equivalent, every Indic workflow pays a hidden tax. A tokeniser built for Indian scripts attacks that tax directly. Note the word "up to": the gain will vary by language and by how much English is mixed into the text.

What did the Chariot-1 benchmarks show?

The developer reports faster training from two techniques and the lowest speech error rates among the systems it tested, but these are company-reported results, not independent evaluations.

Nested Training lets three complete model sizes come out of a single training run and one set of weights, delivering a reported 62% improvement in training speed against training the sizes separately, according to PIB. A second technique, Mixture of Vocabularies, splits the vocabulary into 11 groups, cuts the number of vocabulary entries scored per word by 5.7x and improves training speed by 38% on the same GPUs.

On speech, the release reports over 97% verbalisation accuracy on the Async TTS Pronunciation Benchmark, which has 1,029 real-world sentences and 2,251 instances of non-standard text across 42 categories. The Hindi character error rate was 7.60%, and the average across nine other Indian languages was 6.47%. Verbalisation is the unglamorous part that breaks real deployments: reading an amount, a date or an abbreviation correctly in a payment reminder.

Who is paying for India's foundation models?

Mostly the IndiaAI Mission's compute subsidy, and so far it has gone overwhelmingly to model builders rather than researchers or government users.

The mission was approved in March 2024 with an outlay of ₹10,372 crore. ThePrint analysed 304 approvals on the IndiaAI Compute Portal and found the Centre has committed ₹1,275 crore to subsidise compute, of which nine foundation-model organisations received ₹1,058 crore. Foundation-model approvals account for 83% of the subsidy, and the government has paid 98.9% of their bills. Government departments and PSUs account for 12.5%, with 46.8% of bills paid. Researchers, students and fellows received ₹10.14 crore, or 0.8%, with 39.7% of bills paid.

Most portal users get 40% of the bill paid and cover the remaining 60% themselves, ThePrint reports, while 30 approvals were paid in full and make up 85% of all subsidy. Sarvam AI's subsidy now equals its full bill of ₹246.72 crore. Two providers dominate delivery: Yotta handled 45.6% of the subsidy and E2E Networks 21.4%.

The pipeline is wider than Chariot. A July 2026 Lok Sabha reply reported by Indian Television said 20 sovereign model proposals had been selected, including Sarvam AI's 30 billion and 105 billion parameter models, and that the mission had allocated 93.18 lakh GPU hours.

How much does IndiaAI compute cost?

MeitY told Parliament that its pooled GPUs cost an average subsidised ₹65 an hour, about one-third of the global average.

More than 38,000 GPUs have been onboarded through the centralised compute portal, TelecomTalk reported from a March 2026 Lok Sabha reply. Kashmir Life, covering a Rajya Sabha reply the same month, put the average subsidised cost at ₹65 an hour. ThePrint cites a tender rate of about ₹140 an hour for an H100 before subsidy. The same government reply mentions potential investments of over $250 billion announced at the IndiaAI Impact Summit 2026.

For a private company, the practical point is narrower: subsidised compute mainly goes to model builders and eligible startups. Most operators will consume Indic models through an API or an open-weight download, not train their own.

Should Indian businesses switch to an Indic LLM?

Not by default; test one on your own Indian-language workload and switch only where it beats your current model on accuracy and cost per task.

The case for testing is strong when a large share of your text or voice traffic is in Hindi or regional languages, when token bills are a visible line item, or when data residency matters to your customers or regulator. The case is weaker for English-heavy internal tools, coding work, or complex reasoning, where large global models still set the bar. Availability terms for Chariot-1, including licence, API access and pricing, were not stated in the launch release, so confirm them before planning around it.

How to evaluate Chariot-1 or any Indic LLM: a 6-step checklist

  1. Pick one workflow. A Hindi customer-support queue, Marathi invoice notes, or Tamil voice reminders. One, not five.
  2. Build a 200-item test set from your own data. Real messages, with code-mixed Hinglish, numbers, dates and product names.
  3. Count tokens first. Run the same set through each model's tokeniser. If the 2.5x claim holds even partly on your text, the cost case starts there.
  4. Score accuracy per task, not per benchmark. Did it classify the ticket right, extract the right amount, read the number aloud correctly?
  5. Compare cost per completed task. Tokens times price plus the cost of human correction. A cheaper model that needs more review is not cheaper.
  6. Check deployment terms. Licence, hosting region, data retention, uptime and support. Sovereign is a property of where it runs and who controls it, not only who trained it.

What this means for Indian businesses

Three things. First, the cost of serving Indian languages is starting to fall at the tokeniser level, which is where it was most unfair. Second, India now has several home-grown model families in development, so vendor choice for Indic workloads will widen through 2027. Third, launch-day numbers are developer-reported; the useful number is the one you measure on your own data.

From the way we build, the model is rarely the hard part. The work is in the plumbing: connecting the model to your ERP, CRM or hospital system, routing the right language to the right model, logging every answer and keeping a human in the loop where money or patient care is involved.
