What is an AI governance framework?

An AI governance framework is a written operating model that names who approves AI use, what data each tool may touch, how outputs are checked, and how incidents are handled. It turns "be careful with AI" into decisions someone signs.

A workable framework has five parts: an inventory of AI systems in use, a risk tier for each, controls matched to the tier (access, data, human review, logging), named owners, and a review cycle. It is not a single AI governance policy document. The policy is the rulebook; the framework is the rulebook plus the people, tools and evidence that show the rules are followed.

India's national direction is principle-based rather than a hard law. MeitY's guidelines rest on seven guiding principles, or sutras, including "Do No Harm", fairness, transparency, accountability and privacy, and six pillars of governance covering data stewardship, risk mitigation and regulatory alignment, according to SCC Times. For a company, that means regulators expect you to show your own controls rather than wait for a checklist.

Why do Indian companies need AI governance now?

Because the cost of ungoverned AI is now measurable. IBM's 2025 study puts the average Indian breach at ₹220 million, up about 13% from ₹195 million in 2024, and shadow AI was one of the top three cost drivers.

The gap is not awareness; it is controls. IBM reports that only 37% of Indian organisations have AI access controls, and of those that do have an AI governance policy, only 34% use any technology to enforce it. Policies that live in a PDF do not stop an employee pasting a customer list into a free chatbot.

Regulation is also tightening. The DPDP Rules 2025 are being phased in over 12 to 18 months, and YourStory reports penalties can reach ₹250 crore per breach for data fiduciaries. Gartner predicts that by 2028, AI regulatory violations will drive a 30% increase in legal disputes for tech companies, and that fragmented AI regulation will cover 75% of the world's economies by 2030, per Rocking Robots' report on Gartner research.

How big is the shadow AI problem?

Shadow AI is large enough to show up in breach costs. IBM found it added ₹17.9 million to the average Indian breach, and only 42% of organisations have policies to manage AI or detect shadow AI.

Shadow AI means AI tools used without IT oversight: personal ChatGPT accounts, browser extensions that summarise email, free transcription bots in sales calls. Banning them rarely works. Employees use them because they save time, and the useful response is to offer an approved alternative with logging and data controls.

The attack surface around AI also overlaps with the usual suspects. IBM's India data lists phishing at 18% and third-party vendor and supply chain compromise at 17% as the top two attack vectors. Every AI vendor you plug into your ERP or CRM is a third party, so vendor review belongs inside the AI governance framework, not in a separate procurement silo.

How mature is responsible AI in Indian companies?

Maturity is low and uneven. Nasscom's survey of 574 senior executives found 30% of Indian businesses have mature responsible AI practices and 45% are implementing formal frameworks.

Size matters most. Large corporations lead at 46% maturity, SMEs trail at 20% and startups at 16%, according to the Nasscom report covered by The Tribune. Computer Weekly reports that large enterprises are 2.3x more likely than startups, and 1.5x more likely than SMEs, to report mature practices. By sector, BFSI leads at 35%, technology, media and telecom follow at 31%, and healthcare sits at 18%.

The problems firms actually hit are practical. Nasscom found hallucinations were the most common challenge at 56%, followed by privacy violations at 36%, lack of explainability at 35% and unintended bias at 29%. The top barrier to doing better was a lack of high-quality data, cited by 43%.

Who should own AI governance in a company?

Ownership should sit with the leadership team, with day-to-day control delegated to a small cross-functional group. Nasscom found about 48% of Indian firms place primary responsibility with the C-suite or board.

Another 26% place it with departmental heads, which works for execution but fails when a tool spans finance, HR and operations. Mature organisations formalise this: 65% of them have set up an AI ethics board or similar body, per Nasscom. For a mid-sized firm, that board can be four people: the CIO or IT head, a business owner, legal or compliance, and the person who runs data. It meets monthly, keeps minutes, and approves each new AI use case against its risk tier.

Does governance slow AI adoption down?

Done well, governance speeds adoption because approved patterns stop every project from starting at zero. Gartner research found firms using AI governance platforms were 3.4x more likely to report highly effective governance.

The money is moving the same way. Gartner's 2026 CIO survey found 84% of respondents expect their enterprise to increase GenAI funding this year, and Rocking Robots reports Gartner projects spending on AI data governance will reach $492 million in 2026 and pass $1 billion by 2030. Over 70% of IT leaders told Gartner that regulatory compliance is a top-three challenge for GenAI deployment. Those who solve it first get to scale first.

How to build an AI governance framework: a 90-day playbook

This is the sequence we would use for an Indian mid-market firm with a handful of AI tools in use and no formal policy.

  1. Inventory (weeks 1–2). List every AI tool in use, approved or not: SaaS features, chatbots, copilots, internal models, vendor APIs. Survey teams; check expense claims and browser extensions. Shadow AI is found, not declared.
  2. Tier by risk (weeks 2–3). Classify each use case: low (drafting internal text), medium (customer-facing content, analytics on business data), high (decisions on credit, hiring, patient care, pricing, or anything touching personal data under DPDP).
  3. Write a two-page AI governance policy (week 4). Approved tools, banned data types, human review rules per tier, incident reporting, and who signs off. Short enough that people read it.
  4. Put in technical controls (weeks 4–8). SSO and access controls on AI tools, data-loss rules for uploads, prompt and output logging for medium and high tiers, and an approved enterprise alternative to the free tools people already use.
  5. Review vendors (weeks 6–10). For each AI vendor: where data is stored, whether it trains on your data, breach notification terms, and an exit plan.
  6. Test for the known failure modes (weeks 8–12). Hallucination checks on a fixed test set, bias spot-checks on decisions about people, and an explainability note for every high-tier use case.
  7. Set the cadence (week 12 onward). Monthly AI council review, quarterly policy refresh, and a simple register that records approvals, incidents and fixes. The register is your evidence when a regulator or customer asks.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a law on AI governance in India?

There is no standalone AI law yet. MeitY's India AI Governance Guidelines, released in November 2025, set out seven principles and six pillars and favour a light-touch approach. However, the DPDP Act and its 2025 Rules already apply to any AI system that processes personal data, with penalties of up to ₹250 crore per breach.

What is the difference between an AI governance policy and a framework?

A policy is the written rulebook: approved tools, banned data, review rules. A framework adds the operating model around it: named owners, a risk tier for each use case, technical controls, vendor reviews and a register of approvals and incidents. Without the framework, the policy is rarely enforced.

What is shadow AI?

Shadow AI is any AI tool used at work without IT approval or oversight, such as personal chatbot accounts or browser extensions. IBM found it was among the top three breach cost drivers in India in 2025, adding ₹17.9 million to the average breach, and only 42% of firms have policies to detect it.

How long does it take to set up an AI governance framework?

A mid-sized company can put a working first version in place in about 90 days: two weeks for an inventory, a week for risk tiers, a short policy, then technical controls, vendor reviews and testing. It then needs a monthly review cycle, because AI tools and regulations change faster than annual policy refreshes.

Do small companies need responsible AI governance?

Yes, scaled to their size. Nasscom found only 20% of Indian SMEs and 16% of startups have mature responsible AI practices, against 46% of large firms. A small firm can start with an approved tool list, a ban on uploading personal data, and one named owner who signs off on new AI uses.
